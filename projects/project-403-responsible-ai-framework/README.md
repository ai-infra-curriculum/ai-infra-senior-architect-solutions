# Project 403: Responsible AI Framework

> Senior-architect capstone. The learner submits an enterprise-wide
> Responsible AI framework that a Board-level risk committee, an
> external auditor, and a first-line product team can all work from —
> without one of them silently paying for the other two.

## Scenario (fictional)

**Aurora Life Group** is a fictional multinational life-and-health
insurer headquartered in Amsterdam. The group writes life insurance,
health insurance, and group employer-benefits business in the EU,
the United Kingdom, the United States (state-regulated), Brazil,
India, and Singapore, and operates a reinsurance arm in Bermuda.
Total policy count, premium volume, and headcount are illustrative
only; the scenario exists to force concrete Responsible AI
trade-offs against named regulations.

Aurora has AI in production across three business lines:

1. **Underwriting** — automated life-and-health risk assessment
   models that ingest applicant data, medical evidence, and third-
   party enrichment to produce premium and eligibility decisions.
   Owned by the Chief Underwriting Officer.
2. **Claims** — claims triage and fraud-scoring models that route
   claims to auto-adjudication, human review, or special-
   investigations. Owned by the Chief Claims Officer.
3. **Group benefits / HR services** — as part of the employer-
   benefits proposition, Aurora offers member-facing wellness
   recommenders and, for corporate clients, employee-facing tools
   that adjacent to hiring, promotion, and performance workflows.
   Owned by the Chief Commercial Officer of the Group Benefits
   business unit.

Two independent events force a Responsible AI programme:

- **Regulatory**. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is in
  force. Aurora's underwriting and claims-triage systems are
  substantially in scope of Annex III as high-risk AI systems; the
  employer-benefits toolset is at material risk of being classified
  as high-risk in the employment-and-worker-management category.
- **Reputational**. A peer insurer in an adjacent market has been
  subject to a supervisory action for algorithmic pricing that a
  regulator characterized as unfairly discriminatory. Aurora's
  Board Risk Committee has asked the Chief Risk Officer to
  demonstrate — with evidence, not intent — that the group's
  own systems are defensible.

The Board has approved standing up a **Responsible AI (RAI)
programme** and asked the AI Infrastructure Senior Architect (the
learner) to produce the framework, the AI Ethics Board charter,
the bias-and-explainability standards, the EU-AI-Act compliance
roadmap, and the public transparency reporting model. Funding for
the RAI programme is contingent on this framework passing an
external-counsel and external-audit review.

## The problem statement (what a senior architect is being asked to solve)

Design an enterprise-wide Responsible AI framework such that:

1. **Regulated AI systems are identifiable, inventoried, and
   classified.** A regulator, an internal auditor, or a Board Risk
   Committee member can ask "how many high-risk AI systems does
   Aurora operate, and where?" and get an answer from a single
   named artifact, not from a project-management tool or an
   inbox.
2. **Ethics oversight is real, not performative.** The AI Ethics
   Board has a written charter, defined authority, defined
   quorum, defined dissent-recording process, and a documented
   path from ethics-board decisions to product decisions that
   does not rely on personalities.
3. **Bias and explainability are enforced by the platform, not
   promised by the model owner.** Fairness metrics are declared
   per system before deployment; explainability artifacts are
   generated by the platform as a side-effect of deployment;
   drift on either fairness or explainability triggers a
   defined response.
4. **Transparency reporting is publishable.** A public
   transparency report can be produced on a scheduled cadence
   from the platform's own records, without a bespoke
   engineering effort per cycle. What is public and what is
   private is decided once, in policy.
5. **The regulatory roadmap survives regulatory drift.** The EU
   AI Act's obligations phase in through 2027; NIST AI RMF and
   ISO/IEC 42001 evolve independently; US state laws (Colorado
   SB 21-169, NYC Local Law 144, Illinois HB 3773 and equivalents)
   land unevenly. The framework accommodates all of them at the
   same time without a rewrite each time one moves.
6. **Board-level oversight is architected.** The Board Risk
   Committee and Audit Committee receive a defined dashboard
   at a defined cadence, produced from the same platform records
   as the public transparency report. Board members are not asked
   to trust a slide; they are shown the artifacts.

## Deliverables the learner submits

1. **Responsible AI framework** (`framework/responsible-ai-framework.md`
   or equivalent). The reference artifact this repository ships.
   Includes principles, an AI system taxonomy, the classification
   scheme (EU AI Act aligned, NIST AI RMF aligned), the enforcement
   controls, and the operating model.
2. **AI Ethics Board charter** (`governance/ethics-board-charter.md`
   or equivalent). Composition, quorum, authority, decision rights,
   dissent recording, escalation to Board Risk Committee, standing
   agenda, external-member policy, conflicts-of-interest handling.
3. **Bias and explainability standards** (a section within the
   framework artifact, or standalone). Fairness metric selection
   guidance per use-case class, minimum evidence requirements,
   monitoring cadence, remediation obligations, and the enforcement
   hook in the platform (typically the model registry).
4. **EU AI Act compliance roadmap** (a section within the framework,
   or standalone). Inventory → classification → gap analysis →
   remediation → conformity assessment, sequenced against the Act's
   phased application dates and against Aurora's own change-freeze
   windows.
5. **Public transparency reporting framework** (a section within
   the framework). What is reported, at what cadence, to whom, and
   the mechanism by which the report is produced from platform
   records rather than from a slide deck.
6. **Board-level governance oversight model** (a section within the
   framework or a standalone). Dashboard content, escalation
   thresholds, Board Risk Committee cadence, Audit Committee cadence,
   and the tie-back to the AI Ethics Board.
7. **Risk register** (top 10 responsible-AI risks with probability,
   impact, mitigation, and residual risk). Must include: enforcement
   drift (control degrades quietly), classification error (system
   silently mis-classified as low-risk), regulator inquiry latency
   (evidence takes weeks to produce), and ethics-board capture (the
   board rubber-stamps).

## Explicitly out of scope

- **Model development methodology.** The learner is designing the
  framework a model owner is accountable to, not the modeling
  approach a data scientist uses.
- **Business case in dollars.** RAI is a licence-to-operate concern
  and a reputational-defensibility concern. Value framing is
  appropriate; NPV is the wrong artifact for this project.
  (Contrast with Project 401, which is the finance workstream.)
- **Specific fairness metric formulas.** The framework specifies
  *how* fairness metrics are selected, declared, evidenced, and
  monitored. The choice of a specific metric for a specific model
  is a first-line data-science decision, informed by the framework.
- **Detailed vendor selection for MLOps tooling.** Categories are
  named (model registry, policy engine, monitoring); tool choice
  is deferred to the platform-build phase.
- **Non-AI ethics topics.** Data privacy governance, information
  security governance, and generic corporate ethics are adjacent
  disciplines with their own frameworks. This project cites them
  as neighbours and does not redesign them.

## What "senior architect quality" means for this project

A junior submission gives you a set of principles and an org chart.
A senior submission gives you a framework whose claims a regulator
could test:

- **The classification decision is auditable.** Point to the
  artifact that shows why a system is high-risk and who signed off.
- **The bias claim is verifiable.** Point to the fairness metric
  declared for the system, the evidence generated at the last
  evaluation, and the monitoring signal since.
- **The transparency claim is producible.** Show the report and
  the platform records it was produced from, without an
  engineering escalation.
- **The Board oversight claim is real.** Show the last three
  Board Risk Committee packs and the platform records they were
  produced from, and show at least one instance where the
  dashboard drove a decision.
- **The framework survives leadership rotation.** If the current
  Chief Risk Officer leaves next quarter, nothing in the framework
  breaks. Named-person dependencies are a design defect.

The reference solution in `SOLUTION.md` is not a scoring key with a
single right answer. It is a set of trade-offs a senior architect
should be able to defend on their feet in a Board Risk Committee
session.

## Evaluation

See `rubric/evaluation-rubric.md` for the scoring rubric. The
rubric weights **enforceability of claims** above **breadth of
principles**. A learner who publishes twelve principles but cannot
show any of them are enforced by a control will score below a
learner who publishes five principles and can point at the control
enforcing each one.
